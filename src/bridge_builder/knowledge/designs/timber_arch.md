## How it carries load

A tied arch with the road at the level of its feet. The rib rises from the
anchor at (-6,3) over a crown at y=7 and comes down on the anchor at (6,3).
An arch turns vertical load into compression along the rib, which pushes
outward at the feet; the steel deck between the anchors ties the feet together.
Vertical hangers carry the deck up to the rib.

With pinned joints the rib, the hangers and the deck only make four-sided panels,
and a four-sided panel of pinned beams can shear flat. A real arch resists that
with stiff joints; here diagonal braces do the job. Without them the arch sways
under the moving train and sags about 0.7 m.

Steel goes only where wood would fail. The deck carries the wheels and ties the
arch. The four sloped rib segments are the longest beams pushed hardest: wood
there buckles (the springing segments reach 1.17 of a wood beam's limit). The
crown, the hangers and all the bracing are wood, which works because the
braces share the load between many members.

## Key geometry

- Deck (steel): y=3 from x=-6 to x=6.
- Rib: (-6,3) -> (-4,5) -> (-2,7) -> (0,7) -> (2,7) -> (4,5) -> (6,3).
  The four sloped segments are steel; the two crown beams at y=7 are wood.
- Short hangers (wood): x=-4 and x=4, from y=3 to y=5 (one beam each).
- Long hangers (wood): x=-2, 0, 2, from y=3 to y=7 (two beams each, through (x,5)).
- Braces (wood): an X in each outer panel ((-4,3)<->(-2,5) and (-4,5)<->(-2,3), mirrored),
  a V in the middle ((-2,3)->(0,5)<-(2,3)), and a tie at y=5 from x=-2 to x=2.

## When to choose it

When sustainability matters: 18 of its 28 beams are wood, the highest wood share
of the reference designs, and nothing hangs below the road, so ships can pass.
Wood costs more per beam than steel, so it is not the cheap option. An all-wood
version snaps, and so does one with only a steel deck.
